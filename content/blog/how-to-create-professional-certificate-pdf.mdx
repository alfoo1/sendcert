Most certificates people make at home look homemade. Not because the creator lacked taste, but because a few specific choices are doing the heavy lifting of "expensive" versus "amateur," and they're not obvious until you see them all at once.

Here's what actually separates a professional-looking certificate PDF from one that looks like it was generated in Word at 2am.

## The five decisions that matter

In rough order of impact:

1. **Typography.** Which fonts you use and how you combine them.
2. **Whitespace.** How much empty space you leave around things.
3. **The hierarchy.** Which text is biggest, which is smallest, and why.
4. **The color palette.** How many colors, and how restrained.
5. **The print resolution.** Whether it looks good printed or only on screen.

The good news: you only need to get these five right once. After that, every certificate you issue inherits the same polish.

## 1. Typography

The single most common mistake: using one font for everything, and picking a font meant for body text. Times New Roman at 12pt looks like a term paper. Arial at 14pt looks like a reminder email.

A professional certificate uses **two fonts**:

- **A display font** for the headline ("Certificate of Completion," the recipient name). This should feel authoritative — Playfair Display, Cormorant Garamond, Libre Baskerville, or a classic like Didot or Garamond all work.
- **A body font** for everything else. Keep it simple — Inter, Source Sans Pro, or just system Helvetica is fine.

Avoid:

- Comic Sans. Obviously.
- Papyrus. It's not 2004.
- More than two fonts per certificate.
- Anything called "elegant script" — it's usually illegible at size.

### Size hierarchy

A common working set:

- Recipient name: 48–60pt.
- "Certificate of Completion" headline: 28–36pt.
- Body copy ("awarded to," "for completing"): 18–22pt.
- Footer / signer / date: 14–18pt.

The recipient's name should be the biggest single thing on the page. If the course title is bigger, the composition is wrong — the certificate is about the student, not the course.

## 2. Whitespace

Cheap certificates crowd. Expensive certificates breathe.

A few rules of thumb:

- **Margins:** at least 80–120px from the edge of the page to any content (at 1200×900 pixel canvas).
- **Space around the name:** give it 40–60px of padding above and below. Don't stack text immediately underneath.
- **Don't fill all four corners.** Two corners of ornament is classic. Four is crowded.

If the certificate looks sparse, you're closer than if it looks full. Err toward less.

## 3. The hierarchy

Walk through the content with your eye and make sure the order is right:

1. First, you should notice the recipient name (biggest).
2. Then the headline (second biggest).
3. Then the reason it's being awarded.
4. Then the signature and date.

If your eye lands on the institution name first, it's too big. If the footer disclaimer competes with the headline, it's overdesigned. The name is the subject — everything else is supporting cast.

## 4. Color palette

Three safe patterns:

- **Monochrome.** Black text on cream or white paper. Classic, timeless, cheap to print, never looks wrong.
- **Mono + one accent.** Same as above, but one accent color for the border, dividing lines, and headline. This is the most common "looks expensive" recipe.
- **Full brand.** Your brand's primary color plus one secondary. Use this if your course has strong existing brand identity.

Anti-patterns:

- Gradients. They look cheap on paper and weird in PDFs at print resolution.
- Six colors. Limit yourself to one plus black.
- Pastels on pastels. Low contrast, prints badly.

## 5. Print resolution

A professional certificate should look as good printed as it does on screen. That means:

- **Canvas size:** at least 1200×900 pixels (roughly 4:3 landscape). Larger is fine.
- **DPI on export:** 300 dpi for print quality.
- **Format:** PDF, not JPG. PDFs preserve sharp text; JPGs pixelate when enlarged.
- **No raster-heavy backgrounds.** A low-res background image will look fine on screen and terrible on paper.

If your tool exports PDFs that embed the text as text (vector), the resulting file prints perfectly at any size. If it exports PDFs that rasterize the text (convert it to pixels), you lose print quality. Prefer tools that do vector text — [SendCert](/app) does, for example.

## Adding your logo

A small logo in the top corner anchors the certificate to your brand. Rules:

- Keep it small. 60px tall max at 1200px canvas width.
- Use a transparent PNG or SVG. JPGs with white backgrounds look sloppy.
- Place it top-left or top-right, not dead center (that's where the eye expects the headline).

If your logo is text ("Example Academy Inc."), consider leaving it off and letting the institution name do the work instead.

## Putting it together

A checklist for a certificate that will look professional:

- Two fonts (one display, one body).
- Recipient name is the largest text.
- At least 80px of margin all around.
- One accent color on top of black on cream/white.
- Small logo in one corner (optional).
- Exported as a vector PDF at 1200×900+ pixels.

Most templates that check all these boxes look expensive. Most that miss more than one look homemade.
