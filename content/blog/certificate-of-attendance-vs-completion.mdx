A certificate of attendance says a person was present. A certificate of completion says they finished everything the program required. A certificate of achievement says they met a standard, usually by passing an assessment. Pick the one that matches what you actually checked, because the certificate is a claim about the recipient and it should be true.

The three names get mixed up all the time. Here's how to tell them apart and word each one correctly.

## What is a certificate of attendance?

A **certificate of attendance** confirms that someone was present at an event or session. It makes no claim about what they learned or whether they completed any work.

Use it for:

- Webinars, conferences and talks
- One-off workshops with no assignment or test
- Staff briefings and info sessions

Example wording:

> This certifies that **Jordan Lee** attended *Introduction to Grant Writing*, a 2-hour live session held on March 12, 2026.

Because attendance is the only requirement, base it on real attendance data (a sign-in sheet or your webinar platform's attendance report), not on the registration list. Our guide to [webinar certificates of attendance](/blog/webinar-certificate-of-attendance) covers how to pull that list.

## What is a certificate of completion?

A **certificate of completion** confirms that someone finished all the required parts of a program: every module, session or assignment you defined as mandatory.

Use it for:

- Online courses with several modules
- Multi-session trainings
- Required staff trainings, like safety or child-protection courses

Example wording:

> This certifies that **Jordan Lee** has successfully completed *Child Safety Fundamentals*, a 6-hour training program, on March 12, 2026.

Completion is about finishing, not about scoring. If your course has a quiz that people must pass, consider whether you're really issuing an achievement certificate. For layout tips, see our [free certificate of completion template](/blog/certificate-of-completion-template-free) guide.

## What is a certificate of achievement?

A **certificate of achievement** confirms that someone met a defined standard: passed an exam, scored above a threshold, or produced work that was assessed.

Use it for:

- Courses with a graded final exam or project
- Skills assessments
- Awards and recognition ("top performer", "honors")

Example wording:

> This certifies that **Jordan Lee** passed the *Data Analysis Fundamentals* final assessment with a score of 92%, on March 12, 2026.

Because it claims a result, keep the evidence (scores, rubrics) on file.

## How do the three types compare?

| | Attendance | Completion | Achievement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proves | Presence | Finished all requirements | Met a standard |
| Typical requirement | Showed up | All modules or sessions done | Passed an assessment |
| Key wording | "attended" | "has completed" | "has achieved" / "passed" |
| Evidence to keep | Attendance list | Progress records | Scores or graded work |
| Common use | Webinars, events | Courses, trainings | Exams, awards |

## Which certificate should I issue?

Answer these questions in order:

1. **Did you assess people?** If they had to pass something, issue a certificate of achievement.
2. **Did they have to finish several required parts?** If yes, issue a certificate of completion.
3. **Was being there the only requirement?** Issue a certificate of attendance.

If you are unsure, choose the more modest claim. An attendance certificate that undersells someone is harmless; an achievement certificate for someone who wasn't assessed can cause problems later.

## What about a certificate of participation?

A **certificate of participation** sits between attendance and completion. It says the person took part actively (in a workshop, hackathon, volunteer day or competition) without claiming they finished a curriculum or won anything. It's a good fit for events where contribution matters more than presence alone.

## What should every certificate include?

Whatever the type, include:

- **Recipient's full name**, spelled the way they want it
- **Certificate type**, stated clearly in the title
- **Program or event name**
- **Date** (and duration or hours, if relevant)
- **Issuer**: your organization or your name
- **Signature line**: a typed name or a signature image

For professional credits, add the hours and any approval or course number. Our guide to [continuing education certificates](/blog/continuing-education-certificates) explains what CE boards usually expect.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a certificate of attendance worth anything?

It's proof of presence. Many employers and licensing bodies accept it for training hours, especially if it states the date and duration.

### Can a certificate of completion include a grade?

It can, but then it starts to look like a certificate of achievement. If the grade matters, use "achievement" and state the result.

### Which certificate should I give for a webinar?

Usually a certificate of attendance, based on who actually joined, not who registered.

### Do certificates need a signature?

Not legally in most cases, but a signature line (typed or an image) makes the certificate look official and shows who is vouching for it.
