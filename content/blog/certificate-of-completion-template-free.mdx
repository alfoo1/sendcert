A "certificate of completion" is one of those phrases that sounds stuffy and formal, but what it actually describes is simple: a piece of paper (or PDF) that says "this person finished this thing." The key word is "this person" — a blank certificate with nobody's name on it is not a certificate at all, it is a decorative poster.

If you need a certificate of completion template for your course, workshop, or training, here's what to look for and how to get it working.

## What a certificate of completion needs

At minimum, every certificate of completion should include:

- **Recipient name.** The single most important field. Everything else could vanish and this one still has to be there.
- **The achievement.** What did they complete? The course name, workshop title, or skill demonstrated.
- **Issuer.** Who says they finished? Your school, your company, your name.
- **Date.** When it was completed or when the certificate was issued.
- **Signature line.** Either an actual signature image or a typed name under a line.

That's it. If you have those five fields, you have a legitimate certificate of completion.

## What you do not need

A lot of templates bury the important content under visual noise. Things you can safely leave off:

- **Wax seals, crests, gold foil borders.** If you are not actually a medieval guild, they look silly.
- **Printed serial numbers and QR codes.** They add clutter to a ten-student workshop certificate. If you want people to be able to check a certificate is real, a verification link sent with it does the job; SendCert adds one to every certificate automatically.
- **Lengthy legal disclaimers.** "This certificate is awarded contingent upon…" — save it for the actual diploma.
- **Headshot of the instructor.** Distracting; the instructor's name under a signature is enough.

Simpler is almost always better. The student will look at this for ten seconds before saving the file.

## Design principles for a completion certificate

### 1. The name should be the largest text on the page

This is what the whole document is for. If your course title is bigger than the recipient name, the composition is wrong.

### 2. Use at most two fonts

One display font for the headline ("Certificate of Completion") and one body font for everything else. Three fonts is already pushing it. Five fonts looks like a Word document someone made in 1998.

### 3. Leave room to breathe

Certificates look expensive when they have generous whitespace and cheap when everything crowds the edges. Aim for at least 80–100 px of margin from the edge of the page to anything important.

### 4. Pick one accent color

Black text on white paper is fine. Black text on white paper plus one accent color (for the border, the headline, the dividing line) is better. Using five colors is worse than using one.

## Editable vs printable

"Editable" means you can change the name, date, course title, and so on. "Printable" means the final file is in a format that prints cleanly at standard paper sizes (A4 or US Letter, 300 dpi or better).

You want both. The editable part is the daily work — a different name every time you issue one. The printable part is what happens if a student decides to frame their copy.

A PDF at 1200×900 pixels or larger, exported at print resolution, will satisfy both. Students can view it on screen, print it at home on a color printer, or take it to a print shop for a framing-quality version.

## File format recommendations

- **For email delivery:** PDF. Opens in every email client, looks the same on every device, cannot be accidentally edited.
- **For on-screen sharing (LinkedIn, social):** PDF is fine; PNG works too if you want the student to embed it directly.
- **For print:** PDF, 300 dpi minimum. A4 or US Letter size.

Skip sending DOCX or PPTX. They look different on different machines and half your students will not have the software to open them.
